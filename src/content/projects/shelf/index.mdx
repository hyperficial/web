Read-later apps are good at saving. They are bad at the part after that. The list grows by a few articles a day and shrinks by maybe one a week. After a month it stops being a reading list and turns into an archive of good intentions.

Shelf keeps the save button and changes what you see when you come back: each article as three short lines, written to help you decide, not to replace the article.

## The problem

I looked at a month of my own saves. Most of the articles I never opened weren't bad. I just couldn't tell, from a headline and a domain, whether one was worth twenty minutes. So I opened none of them.

What I wanted was the thing a friend gives you when they send a link: *"read this, it's about X, the good part is Y."*

> The goal is a faster no, and a more confident yes.

## Design

Every screen is the list. There is no inbox, no tags and no folders to sort into. A summary appears where the article already is, so nothing moves while you read.

## The three lines

The summaries carry the whole product, so they have firm rules:

1. **Three lines, always.** Never two, never four. Same shape every time, so the list reads like a table.
2. **Each line is a claim.** No *"the article discusses"*. If a line could be the start of any article, it gets rewritten.
3. **The last line is the reason to read it.** The most surprising or most useful point goes last, because that is where the eye stops.

The prompt asks for these rules and a small check rejects any summary that breaks them, for example too many lines or a line that starts with *"This article"*. A rejected summary is generated again, once.

```ts
const generic = /^(this|the) article/i

const ok = (lines: string[]) =>
	lines.length === 3 &&
	lines.every((l) => l.length < 110 && !generic.test(l))
```

## Summarize all

Summaries are made when you look, not when you save. That keeps the cost proportional to reading, and lets old saves catch up in one tap.
